# Visual Design

Create intentional visual communication through composition, relationships, rhythm,
hierarchy, and character. Shape the artifact around its content, audience, purpose,
and intended experience.

## Establish Context and Direction

Identify the artifact and viewing conditions. Use representative content, including
its actual length, variation, density, and relationships. Ground visual choices in
the subject and audience so the result feels specific to this work.

Determine what should be preserved, evolved, or explored:

- Preserve: work within the established visual language and improve its execution.
- Evolve: retain recognizable strengths while reconsidering weaker choices.
- Explore: develop a new direction or substantially rethink the existing one.

Apply these choices to individual aspects when useful. A project may preserve its
identity and typography while exploring a new composition. Use supplied brand guidance,
references, and existing systems as context. Keep established visual meanings consistent.

Scale exploration to the task. A focused change may need a local adjustment; a new
artifact may benefit from comparing directions. Exploration can produce a restrained
result, and fidelity can still require considerable craft.

## Compose the Whole

Establish regions, relationships, balance, and the movement of attention. Choose
components and treatments to express that composition.

Use position, scale, spacing, alignment, typography, color, imagery, and shape as active
tools. Consider individual regions, the whole artifact, and any sequence of pages or
screens. Give elements weight according to their role and support emphasis with quieter
areas. Allow multiple entry points when browsing or comparison calls for them.

For applications, identify the capabilities that make the product useful, the workflows
connecting them, and the actions within those workflows. Consider importance, frequency,
and consequences when allocating space, emphasis, and persistent visibility. An
infrequent but critical action may still need clear presence. Keep supporting capabilities
discoverable and reveal detail where it becomes relevant.

## Express Relationships Visually

Start with proximity, alignment, spacing, and shared positioning when they communicate
the relationship clearly. Introduce dividers, borders, surfaces, and containers when
they improve grouping, separation, state recognition, interaction, emphasis, or expression.
Choose their strength according to purpose.

Treat empty space as active structure: establish groups, create pauses, shape rhythm,
and give an element presence. Consistent treatments should communicate consistent roles;
deliberate differences should help the viewer understand something.

## Shape Typography and Reading

Choose typography for reading and character. Establish roles through scale, weight,
spacing, and placement. Tune line length, line spacing, and density to the content,
medium, and viewing conditions. Inspect real headings, paragraphs, labels, and values
at their intended size.

Use expressive type where it strengthens the composition. Give supporting text enough
presence to perform its job. Let adjacent copy contribute distinct information while
retaining labels and explanations needed for understanding and confident action.
Propose substantive copy changes through the writing workflow when relevant.

## Spend Attention Deliberately

Give visible elements useful roles: information, orientation, action, emphasis,
atmosphere, or delight. Choose density according to the task. Preserve detail that
enables useful reading or comparison, and create room where attention needs to settle.

Prefer one effective expression of a relationship over redundant signals. Add
reinforcement when it improves comprehension or accessibility. Spend complexity where
it benefits the experience, preserving useful context and richness as you simplify.

## Build Rhythm and Character

Establish coherence through typography, spacing, color, shape, and image treatment.
Vary scale, density, and composition according to content. Use repetition to establish
expectations and deliberate departures to create emphasis, pacing, or surprise.

Choose imagery, illustration, texture, and ornament for their contribution to meaning
and experience. Treat image selection and cropping as part of composition. Use motion
where timing or movement clarifies change, directs attention, or contributes expression;
match its intensity to its role and respect reduced-motion needs.

Preserve intentional character as the design becomes clearer. Successful work can be
sparse or dense, quiet or vivid, utilitarian or decorative.

## Design for Actual Use

Account for viewing size, content variation, and relevant states. Maintain legibility,
sufficient contrast, and recognizable actions. Support important meanings with more
than color alone. Preserve useful information and relationships as presentation changes.

## Inspect and Refine in Both Directions

Inspect the rendered artifact at its intended size and in relevant viewing conditions.
Review the whole composition and details. For interfaces, inspect representative sizes,
content, and states; for sequences and paginated work, inspect continuity as well.

- Where does attention go, and does it serve the intended experience?
- Are relationships, content, and actions understandable?
- What competes unnecessarily or repeats an existing job?
- What needs more presence, detail, contrast, or room?
- Does the composition have appropriate balance, rhythm, and variation?
- Are choices grounded in the content and chosen direction?
- Has simplification weakened character, context, or usability?
- Does the result work in its actual medium and viewing conditions?

Remove what weakens the composition and strengthen what deserves more presence.
Inspect the revision. When rendering or relevant viewing conditions are unavailable,
identify what remains visually unverified.
