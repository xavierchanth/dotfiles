# Narrative and Message Architecture

Establish the underlying substance, then apply it to the audience and purpose, then
choose the piece's section architecture. Reuse settled foundations and scale depth
to the task. These structures are starting questions, not mandatory headings.

## Narrative Foundation

- What is the central idea, experience, or understanding?
- What context makes it meaningful?
- What tension, question, observation, or opportunity motivates it?
- How do the supporting ideas connect?
- What changes if the reader understands or adopts this?
- What meaning should remain consistent across expressions?

The foundation establishes substance without prescribing an opening or a story arc.

## Narrative Approaches

Choose or combine approaches based on the source material. The elements describe
relationships to develop; their order in the finished piece can vary.

| Approach | Useful elements |
|---|---|
| Company | Thesis, problem, why now, solution, mechanism and proof, why us |
| Point of view | Observation, position, reasoning, implications |
| Experience and reflection | Situation, experience, interpretation, changed understanding |
| Framework | Recurring challenge, organizing principle, elements and relationships, application |
| Instructional | Desired understanding or outcome, starting knowledge, progression, successful application |

### Company Narrative

Keep the argument distinct from the market context used to apply it.

The argument establishes the thesis, problem, timing, proposed solution, how it works,
supporting proof, and why this company is positioned to realize it.

Market context can establish core strategy, positioning, initial focus and expansion,
packaging, distribution, and buyer cases. Add financing strategy when relevant to
investor work. Use existing material and surface unresolved questions; a company
template should not be imposed on unrelated personal or instructional writing.

## Message Architecture

Apply the narrative to a particular audience, purpose, and channel. Keep this simple:
short answers may suffice, and fields without a useful role can be omitted.

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Audience | Primary readers and relevant secondary audiences |
| Purpose | Why the communication exists |
| Desired takeaway | What should remain with the reader |
| Core message | The central idea expressed for this audience |
| Content themes | Supporting ideas to develop |
| Support | Evidence, experience, examples, or demonstrations |
| Resistance | Relevant misunderstandings, questions, or disagreement |
| Next step, when relevant | What the audience should do afterward |

Distinguish purpose, takeaway, and core message: intended effect, retained
understanding, and the actual message. A piece can succeed without a call to action.

Use resistance to improve understanding and expose fit. Address questions that help
receptive people understand or act; treat fundamental disagreement as useful
qualification instead of allowing objections to dominate the piece.

Use this architecture to assign section roles, emphasis, framing, and supporting
depth. A linear narrative, multiple entry points, or progressive explanation may each
serve the purpose. Choose deliberately.

## Language and Claims Across Pieces

When several pieces share a narrative, maintain the terminology and meaning that
should stay consistent. Capture the following where useful:

- Canonical terms and their meanings.
- Preferred expressions that preserve the intended voice and framing.
- Core claims and the support available for them.
- Claims needing qualification or additional evidence.
- Language that would misrepresent the intended meaning.

Adapt emphasis, depth, and phrasing to the audience. Keep proposed positioning,
demonstrated capability, personal observation, and ambition distinguishable. Preserve
conviction while representing the substance accurately.
